When a physicist says some quantity is "given by" a piece of math, a cohomology class, a modulus, an index, what is that arrow from math to measurement really doing? And how confident should we be in it?

Usually nobody says. The step from mathematical object to physical reality gets left as an unstated assumption you are supposed to swallow.

Part I of a new modular seven paper series takes that step seriously. It builds what we call a representation stack, a pipeline that picks up a mathematical object, sends it through a named physical channel, and reads off an observable number: Obs(M) = Obs(Real(Φ(M))).

Every step in that pipeline wears a confidence label, either Standard, Heuristic, or Speculative, and we prove the labels behave the way intuition says they should. Confidence can only drop as you chain translations together, never climb. There is no free epistemic lunch.

The point is to give researchers a shared, checkable way to be honest about how solid a "math equals physics" claim actually is, instead of quietly assuming it. And it is deliberately modular, not unified: each of the next six papers builds its own mathematical faculty on top of this one.

The paper has been peer reviewed, code reviewed, and its core structures are verified in both Haskell and Lean.
